# MARKET RESEARCH — Branded Merchandise, Workwear & PPE in Sub-Saharan Africa and Botswana

**As at:** 15 October 2026 · **Method:** desk research on public sources; no primary fieldwork conducted.
**Reading rule:** every figure carries a confidence label. Market-research publishers disagree materially with each other (see §1.4), so treat all headline numbers as *direction*, not *precision*. Where Botswana-specific data does not exist, I build bottom-up estimates and mark them clearly.

---

## 1. Global and continental picture

### 1.1 Global promotional products / branded merchandise

- **United States (the most rigorously measured market):** US$27.1bn in 2025 distributor sales, **+1.3%** year-on-year — growth below inflation, so real-terms contraction. Costs (tariffs 56.8%, freight 54.3%, client budget cuts 50.6%) squeezed margins; ~90% of distributors raised prices in 2025 [S3].
- **Category shifts to watch:** "retail branded products" (recognisable consumer brands used as gifts) grew far above industry average to ~US$6bn; sustainable products reached ~US$3.8bn (14% of sales) [S3].
- **Structural shift toward non-industry sourcing:** US$4.3bn (16% of industry sales) came from suppliers outside the traditional promo network — i.e. distributors are buying from general wholesalers, importers and marketplaces. This is evidence that **the supplier-network moat is eroding**, which favours an orchestrator who sources wherever is best [S3].
- **Sentiment 2026:** 60% of distributors expect higher sales but only 53% expect higher profit; mid-2026 flash data showed distributors +2.3%, suppliers +2.1% [S3][S4].
- Confidence: **Known** (PPAI is the industry's authoritative measurement body). Note: PPAI's own data is a survey-based estimate, directionally accurate rather than exact.

### 1.2 Africa

- One commercial publisher puts the **African promotional products market at US$5.045bn (2021) → US$6.373bn (2025) → US$9.262bn (2033)**, a ~4.8% CAGR, and describes Africa as the **fastest-growing region**, led by South Africa (~3.0% of the global market) and Nigeria (~2.3%) [S1].
- Drivers cited: youth population growth, mobile/internet penetration, expanding multinational corporate presence, telecoms and banking sector expansion. Restraints: infrastructure deficits, political instability in some markets, **fragmented distribution networks** [S1].
- Confidence: **Uncertain.** This is a single secondary publisher; another source in the same search cluster quoted a South African market figure of "US$55.68m" which is internally inconsistent with the regional numbers and clearly unreliable. Use the African figures as indicative order of magnitude only. **Requires validation** if used for an investment case.

### 1.3 South Africa — the best-documented African market

The ASI/Amrod study (published May 2025, 1,311 distributor-owner respondents, one per company) is the single most useful African dataset available:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Distributors reporting 2024 revenue above 2019 | 56% (≈24% lower) |
| Average distributor markup, 2024 | **31.6%** (US average 37%) |
| Markup by size | Extra-large (>R20m) 33.4% · Large (R5–20m) 32.5% · Mid (R1–5m) 32.9% · Small (<R1m) 30.9% |
| Size distribution | <R1m 42% · R1–5m 30% · R5–20m 11% · >R20m 4% |
| Merch is primary business | 65% overall; >80% among >R20m firms |
| Tenure | 20+ yrs 17% · 11–19 yrs 27% · 7–10 yrs 20% · 4–6 yrs 20% · ≤3 yrs 16% |
| Top-selling products | Golf shirts and T-shirts |
| Leading end-buyer market | Education |

Sources: [S2]. Confidence: **Known** for the study's own numbers; the interpretation (fragmentation, thin margins, small-firm dominance) is **Likely**.

**What this means for you:** the South African market has thousands of small distributors earning ~31% markup on product, and almost no industry trade show since COVID — Amrod's own summit (≈800 distributor clients) became the de facto forum [S2]. Capability, not catalogue, is the differentiator. Botswana is a less mature version of the same structure, with one extra advantage: **a policy regime that pays for local value addition** (see §4).

### 1.4 Market-sizing caution

Published figures for the same thing differ by 2× or more. For South African PPE: one publisher says US$957m in 2025, another US$567m in 2024; for Middle East & Africa PPE, US$8.2bn in 2024 growing to US$14.5bn by 2033; global PPE ranges from US$79bn to US$91bn for 2025 depending on publisher [S8][S9][S10][S11]. The direction is consistent — **mid-single-digit to low-double-digit growth, with Africa/MEA the fastest-growing region** — the levels are not. Never put a single publisher's number in a business plan without cross-checking.

Sanity check: this puts a successful Botswana orchestrator at roughly the size of a mid-tier South African distributor (R5–20m band, where average markup is 32.5% [S2]) — credible, not fantastical.

## 4. Why Botswana specifically is attractive right now

**4.1 Policy pays for local value addition.** Government "prioritises locally based manufacturers and service providers where goods and services are available, competitively priced, and meet BOBS requirements"; mining procurement carries a 10% preference for locally produced supplies; contracts below ~US$900k are reserved for citizen-owned businesses; the Minister sets preference margins based on ownership, location, labour source and subcontracting [S21]. Earlier MITI policy set a 15% preferential price margin for small locally based firms, 10% mid, 5% large, across 27 categories including textiles [S22][S23].

> **Design implication:** your tendering strategy should maximise *Botswana value addition* — local decoration, local kitting, local finishing, local assembly — and document it. That is worth 5–15% of price on qualifying tenders, which is often more than the entire net margin on the job.

**4.2 Macro inflection.** Two years of recession (2024, 2025) are turning: Q1 2026 +3.5%, Q2 2026 +8.7% year-on-year, mining output +81.7% [S14][S15]. Corporate budgets reopen in a recovery; capital projects (Khoemacau, Karowe, Jwaneng, Motheo) are mid-cycle, not finishing [S46][S47].

**4.3 Fiscal caution.** The state's own finances are tight — deficit projected at 8.9% of GDP in February 2026, revised to 3.1% in September 2026 with one-off measures; sovereign downgrades to BBB− (S&P) and Baa2 (Moody's) [S12][S13][S14]. Expect: slower government payment, sharper tender competition, and pressure on discretionary government gifting. **Plan for 60–90 day government receivables** and price accordingly.

**4.4 Import cost structure.** Botswana is inside SACU: goods from South Africa, Namibia, Lesotho and Eswatini enter free of customs duty, while extra-union clothing attracts **40–45% MFN duty** (knitted garments average 41.3%) plus **14% VAT** [S30][S31][S19]. This has three consequences:
1. Direct Chinese sourcing for apparel is heavily taxed — you must model duty-inclusive landed cost per unit, never FOB price.
2. Sourcing from Amrod/Barrod-type South African suppliers usually beats direct import on *total* landed cost for small and mid volumes, because the duty is already embedded upstream and you avoid clearance friction, MOQ risk and 6–10 week lead times.
3. Where direct import wins is on high-run, plain, high-margin items (e.g. 3,000 plain tees, gazebos, drinkware, tech gifts) — which is precisely why the model must be able to switch sources per line item rather than committing to one channel.

**4.5 Compliance friction is a moat for whoever masters it.** BOBS operates a dual compulsory/voluntary standards regime; garments are among the products under compulsory standards, and the Standards (Compulsory Standards) Regulations (effective 1 April 2024) require inspection in the country of origin and a **Certificate of Conformity before goods arrive** [S28][S29][S30]. Most small importers do not know this. A supplier who handles CoC, clearing, and HS classification properly, and *shows the paperwork*, is differentiated in exactly the accounts you want.

**4.6 Payments infrastructure improving but still a friction point.** Botswana's VAT regime is standardising (remote-services VAT, electronic fiscal devices coming) [S20]; instant cross-border payments into Botswana went live in March 2026 via the TCIB scheme with First Capital Bank, connecting Botswana to South Africa and Zimbabwe [S48]. Practical effect: collecting from regional customers is getting easier — useful for Namibia/Zambia/Zimbabwe expansion later, not a 2026 priority.

## 5. Segment-level demand evidence (what buyers actually buy)

- **Top products in South Africa:** golf shirts and T-shirts; **education** is the leading end-buyer market [S2]. Expect Botswana to skew similarly but with *higher* relative weight on workwear/PPE because of mining and construction.
- **Global category signals:** retail-branded goods and sustainable products are the growth pockets [S3]. In Botswana terms: recognisable-brand drinkware/tech gifts for executive gifting; recycled/eco materials for ESG-reporting corporates and mining.
- **Decoration technology shift:** short-run digital (DTF/DTG) has collapsed the minimum order barrier — no setup, viable from 12 pieces, per-piece consumables of roughly US$0.50–2.50 [S42][S43]. Screen printing still wins above ~48–100 pieces on simple designs; embroidery dominates left-chest logos on polos, caps and workwear [S42][S45].
- **Industry data standards:** 500+ suppliers now publish PromoStandards endpoints; distributors using them report ~20–30% reductions in order-processing time [S40]. This is the single most under-exploited technology lever in Africa: no Botswana supplier or distributor currently exposes machine-readable product/pricing/inventory data, and none needs to — because nobody has asked. The orchestrator who builds a structured catalogue first gains a data monopoly at the small-market level (see `05-digital-asset-blueprint.md`).

## 6. Gaps in the evidence base (be explicit with lenders and partners)

1. **No Botswana market size** exists for this sector. Our TAM/SAM/SOM is a bottom-up estimate.
2. **Public financials for Botswana competitors are unavailable** (private companies, no filings published). Competitor analysis in `02-competitive-map.md` is capability-based, not revenue-based.
3. **Import duty treatment varies by HS line** (40% vs 45% + any specific duties). We have not verified line-by-line classification for each product family; a clearing agent must confirm [S30][S31].
4. **Current corporate tax rate must be confirmed** — the 22%→24.5% increase was proposed in the 2026/27 budget and carried in the Income Tax Bill; effective date and final rate need confirmation from an auditor or BURS [S16][S17].
5. **OHS reform status in Botswana is unclear** — Botswana still relies on the Factories Act (1973), Mines/Quarries/Works & Machinery Act and Workers Compensation Act rather than a dedicated OHS Act [S33][S34][S52]. If a dedicated OHS Act is enacted, PPE enforcement and demand both rise. Verify with the Ministry of Labour.
6. **Customer willingness-to-pay data is absent.** This is why the 90-day plan starts with 12 buyer interviews, not with building.

## 7. What this research changes about the plan

| Finding | Decision it forces |
|---|---|
| Africa's promo market is fragmented with thousands of sub-R5m distributors [S1][S2] | Do not compete on catalogue breadth. Compete on orchestration, speed, compliance and digital ordering. |
| B2B distribution startups on the continent destroyed capital [S37][S38][S39] | Do not own stock depth or a delivery fleet. Own decoration and the orchestration layer; rent supply and logistics. |
| Local-content preferences worth 5–15% create a structural pricing advantage [S21][S22][S23] | Build local value addition (decorate, kit, assemble, finish) and document it for every tender. |
| 40–45% duty on clothing means sourcing channel choice per item is a margin decision [S30][S31] | Build a duty-aware quote engine and maintain three supply channels (SA trade, direct import, local manufacture). |
| Public data is thin | Make data an asset: build Botswana's branded-supply price index and tender radar as owned properties (`05`). |
| Mining and project pipeline is multi-year and non-discretionary [S46][S47] | Prioritise programme supply contracts over transactional orders; design the PPE/uniform programme product now. |
